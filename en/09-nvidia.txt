By default the driver drops GPU clocks as soon as a scene gets light and raises them with a delay — when the load spikes, that shows up as frame-time hitches. “Prefer maximum performance” keeps clocks high, but only while this game runs: on the desktop the card still saves power.
Not an NVIDIA GPU — these settings are for the NVIDIA driver only.
2
Low latency mode
The CPU can prepare frames ahead and queue them for the GPU — that adds delay between mouse movement and the picture. Low latency mode “On” limits the queue to one frame. It helps most when the game is GPU-bound. If the game has NVIDIA Reflex, enable it in-game: it works better and overrides this setting. Cost: in CPU-bound games FPS may drop by a couple of frames.
3
Frame cap 3 below the monitor's refresh rate
With G-SYNC or FreeSync the monitor follows the game — but only while FPS stays below its refresh rate. Once FPS hits the ceiling, regular VSync kicks in with its latency (or tearing). Capping 3 frames below the refresh rate keeps the game inside the VRR range: smooth picture, minimal latency. The driver applies it to this game only.
4
Shader cache of at least 10 GB
Games compile shaders the first time they meet an effect — hence stutters “in new places”. The driver saves compiled shaders to disk, but the default space is limited, and in big games old shaders get evicted — compilation and stutters repeat. 10 GB fits several big games. The setting is global for all games.
WinPulse doesn't disable Defender or Windows services, doesn't touch HPET, Win32PrioritySeparation or network settings, doesn't clean the registry and never sets games to Realtime priority — these 'tweaks' have no proven effect.
